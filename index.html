Introduction

The Apple as a System of Forms

About the collection

This publication treats the apple not as a single image, but as a system of forms revealed through comparison, cutting, handling, and change over time.

An apple is one of the easiest objects to recognize and one of the easiest to stop looking at. Its outline is familiar, its uses are ordinary, and its varieties are encountered so often that difference can disappear into habit. This publication slows that recognition down. It treats the apple as a form that can be measured, compared, named, cut, handled, stored, and observed over time. The aim is not to build a complete encyclopedia of apples. Instead, the book asks what becomes visible when one familiar object is examined through a small set of repeated actions.

The publication begins with form. Michele Warmund’s “Apple Fruit Shapes” describes fruit shape as one of several characteristics used to identify apple cultivars and lists common categories including round, conic, oblate, oblique, oblong, and ovate. The article also notes that mature apples can be cut longitudinally through the core to categorize shape, and that growing location and environmental conditions can alter the final form. These ideas establish a method for the first part of the book: looking carefully at outline, proportion, and difference rather than assuming that every apple has the same shape.

The second part moves from form to variety. The New York Apple Association presents apples through names, flavor descriptions, textures, colors, parentage, and uses, while Penn State Extension’s “Let’s Preserve: Apples” compares varieties through characteristics such as sweet, tart, firm, crisp, and juicy. Together, these sources show that variety can be read through more than appearance. An apple can be recognized through the combination of what it looks like, how it feels, how it tastes, and how it performs when prepared or preserved.

The final sections follow the apple after its surface is interrupted. Penn State’s preservation guide describes washing, peeling, coring, slicing, and preventing discoloration. Its fruit-rot guidance explains why bruised or wounded apples require careful handling after harvest. University of Minnesota Extension describes storage disorders, dehydration, shrinking, flesh browning, and the importance of temperature and humidity. These sources shift the book from classification toward change.

The physical structure of the book follows that premise. Square interior pages open outward from the center, allowing images and text to be separated, compared, and rejoined across a spread. Some spreads carry dense text; others use images as pauses. The repeated grid, typography, and illustration system hold those different reading speeds together. The publication therefore uses the apple as both subject and method: one ordinary object, repeatedly reconsidered.

Collected and organized in New York, the material is intentionally selective. New York is not presented as the explanation for the apple; it is the setting in which the acts of collecting and comparing take place. The book moves from recognition to attention: from the whole fruit to named shapes, from cultivar differences to cut sections, and from a fresh surface to the visible effects of handling and storage. Each section asks the reader to notice a different kind of evidence.

A cut shifts attention from the skin of the apple to the structure it contains. Inside, the apple is reorganized as a section. Flesh, core, seed pockets, and symmetry come forward, replacing the single outer silhouette with a readable internal order.

Cut

From Whole to Section

The act of cutting appears in more than one of the five sources, but it serves different purposes. In Michele Warmund’s “Apple Fruit Shapes,” a mature apple is cut longitudinally in half through the core so that its shape can be categorized. In Penn State Extension’s “Let’s Preserve: Apples,” cutting belongs to preparation: apples are washed, peeled, cored, and sliced before freezing or canning. In both cases, the whole fruit becomes easier to examine or use after it is divided.

A longitudinal cut passes through the core and turns the apple into a section. Warmund’s description is brief, but the operation is visually important. The outside silhouette is no longer the only information available. The cut creates a central plane through which the relation between the two sides of the fruit can be seen.

For this publication, the longitudinal section becomes a transition between the earlier study of shape and the later study of preparation. The whole apple presents contour; the cut apple presents contour and interior at the same time. The skin becomes an edge around the section rather than the only visible surface.

The purpose is not to invent a new classification beyond the source. Instead, the publication uses the cut to make the source’s method visible. A form that was previously read from the outside is opened so that its internal organization can participate in comparison.

Peel, Core, Slice

Penn State’s preservation guide gives a more procedural sequence. For several preparations, apples are washed, peeled, cored, and sliced. For freezing sliced apples, the guide instructs the reader to wash, peel, and core the fruit, then divide medium apples into twelfths and large apples into sixteenths. For canned sliced apples, it specifies wedges about one-half inch thick.

These instructions introduce scale and repetition. One apple becomes many pieces, and the pieces are made similar enough to be handled consistently. The whole fruit is no longer the final unit. The slice becomes the working unit.

That change is useful for the book’s visual structure. A whole apple occupies one compact area. A series of wedges or slices can be repeated, aligned, separated, or compared. Cutting therefore changes both the material object and the possibilities for organizing it on the page.
