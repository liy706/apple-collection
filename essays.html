Form

How an Apple Becomes a Shape

Measure

Measurement is useful here not because every apple needs to be reduced to a number, but because proportion makes comparison possible. Width and height can make two fruits with similar color read differently. A broader apple may appear compact; a taller apple may seem elongated; a flatter apple may distribute its volume horizontally. The publication uses diameter, height, and stem position as visual guides. These measurements are not presented as a universal standard for all apples. They function as a way to slow down recognition and make proportion visible on the page.

A measurement line does something different from a photograph or illustration. It asks the reader to compare one edge with another. The apple becomes a relationship between dimensions rather than a single silhouette. When several measured forms are placed together, the eye starts to notice where the widest point sits, how sharply the sides taper, and whether the form feels centered or uneven. These observations are editorial rather than diagnostic: they organize what can be seen.

Shape

Warmund’s six categories provide the clearest external structure for this section. A round apple approaches a balanced, circular impression. A conic form narrows from a broader upper area toward the lower part of the fruit. An oblate form is visibly flattened. An oblique form is uneven rather than bilaterally balanced. An oblong form reads as elongated, while an ovate form suggests an egg-like profile. The publication does not treat these terms as decorative labels. They are a compact language for describing differences that otherwise remain vague.

The source pairs these categories with named cultivars: Rome Beauty is shown as round, Braeburn as conic, Redfree as oblate, York Imperial as oblique, and Red Delicious as oblong.

Round
Nearly symmetrical in all directions.
Conic
Wider at the top, tapering toward the base.
Oblate
Flattened vertically with a wider diameter.
Oblique
Uneven rather than bilaterally balanced.
Oblong
Elongated, taller than it is wide.
Ovate
Elongated with a gentle taper toward one end.

These examples are useful because they connect an abstract word to a specific fruit. A shape category becomes easier to understand when it is attached to an actual cultivar rather than a perfect geometric diagram.

At the same time, the categories do not make the apple rigid. Warmund notes that fruit shape may differ by growing location because of environmental conditions. The article gives Red Delicious as an example: fruit ripened where evening temperatures are cool can be more elongated than fruit ripened in warmer evening conditions. Spring frosts and pests can also alter shape. This means that a category is not the same thing as an identical template. A cultivar can still vary.

Variation

That distinction is important to the visual logic of the book. Classification and variation are not opposites. Classification makes variation easier to see. Once a reader understands the general idea of round, conic, or oblate, smaller departures from those forms become more noticeable. One fruit may lean slightly. Another may carry more weight on one side. Another may appear compressed around the middle. The category gives the eye a reference point; the individual fruit interrupts it.

This is why repetition matters. A single apple can appear complete on its own, but a row of apples produces a different kind of information. Similarity establishes a family, while difference gives each member specificity. The repeated apple forms in this publication are therefore not intended as decoration. They create a field for comparison.

The New York Apple Association’s variety archive reinforces this idea in a different way. Its descriptions repeatedly combine visual and sensory characteristics. Cameo is described as bright and red-striped, firm and crisp, with thin skin and a sweet/tart flavor. Gala is described through a bright yellow-red color and a mild flavor. RubyFrost is described as deep and rich, with a hearty crunch and crisp texture. These descriptions show that an apple’s identity is rarely carried by outline alone. Shape is one visible layer within a larger combination of traits.

Variation, then, is not noise around a core form. It is part of the system itself. By showing multiple apples side by side, the repeated forms make small differences legible. They help the reader see how color, proportion, and surface relate, and how many kinds of apples can exist within a shared visual language.

Compare

Comparison is the final operation of this first feature. It connects measurement, shape, variation, and section. One apple alone can be named, but several apples make a system. A round example clarifies an oblong one. A whole fruit clarifies a longitudinal section. A consistent grid clarifies small changes because the surrounding conditions remain stable.

The publication uses this logic to move away from the idea of a single ideal apple. The sources do not present one universal form. Instead, they describe categories, cultivars, and conditions that produce visible differences.

The design follows the same principle. Repetition creates a common frame, and the differences inside that frame become the content. Form, then, is not treated as a fixed outline. It is a way of organizing observation. An apple can be measured without becoming only data, classified without becoming identical to every other example, and cut without losing its recognizable identity.

The first feature establishes a vocabulary for the pages that follow: proportion, category, difference, section, and comparison. These terms prepare the reader to move from external form toward variety, use, and change.

Variety

Reading Difference

If form gives the apple an external vocabulary, variety expands that vocabulary into color, texture, flavor, use, and season. The New York Apple Association’s variety archive does not describe apples as interchangeable. Each entry combines a name with a small set of characteristics: sweet or tart, crisp or soft, juicy or firm, mild or aromatic, useful for snacking, baking, cooking, sauce, or salads. Penn State Extension’s “Let’s Preserve: Apples” adds another structured comparison through a table that marks varieties according to sweet, tart, firm, crisp, and juicy qualities. Read together, the two sources turn variety into a system of difference.

A cultivar name is the first layer of organization. Gala, Granny Smith, Honeycrisp, McIntosh, Empire, Crispin, Braeburn, Fuji, Rome, and Golden Delicious all remain recognizably apples, yet the sources describe them through different combinations of traits. The name becomes the point where appearance, taste, texture, parentage, availability, and use can meet.

The New York Apple Association describes Gala as a mild snacking apple with a bright yellow-red color. Granny Smith is crisp and juicy with a balance of tart and sweet, and is presented as useful for baking, sautéing, and snacking. Honeycrisp is described as honey sweet with a touch of tart and notably crisp. McIntosh is juicy, tangy, and aromatic. Braeburn is sweet, tangy, and juicy. Crispin is associated with crunch and sweet juice. Empire is described as a sweet-tart combination and as versatile. These phrases are short, but together they create different identities.

Penn State’s variety table provides another way to read those identities. It marks Honeycrisp as sweet, firm, crisp, and juicy; Braeburn as sweet, tart, firm, crisp, and juicy; Granny Smith as tart, crisp, and juicy; McIntosh as tart, crisp, and juicy; Empire as tart, firm, and crisp; and Crispin as sweet, firm, and juicy. The table does not tell a narrative. Its value is comparison. The same five descriptors repeat from row to row, so difference appears through pattern.

Color and Surface

Color is one of the fastest ways to distinguish apple varieties on the page. The New York Apple Association describes Cameo as bright and red-striped with a creamy orange ground, Gala as bright yellow-red, RubyFrost as deep and rich, and Golden Delicious through its yellow identity. These descriptions do not create a complete color taxonomy, but they support the visual fact that cultivar identity can be read through skin appearance.

This distinction matters because the written sources repeatedly combine visual and nonvisual information. Gala’s color appears beside its mild flavor. Cameo’s striped surface appears beside firmness, crispness, and sweet/tart flavor. RubyFrost’s visual description appears beside crunch and texture. The identity of a variety is built from combinations.

The publication uses color comparatively rather than symbolically. Red, green, yellow, and blushed surfaces are placed next to one another so that hue works together with the written variety name. The images do not claim that every red apple tastes the same or every green apple is tart.

Surface also carries meaning. A smooth, shiny skin can signal a different eating experience than a more matte or finely textured one. Some varieties are solid in color, while others show stripes, blushes, or gradients that reveal how sunlight and growing conditions shape the fruit. Taken together, color and surface provide a quick but meaningful way to read differences among apple varieties.

Taste

Sweetness, Acidity, Texture

Taste is difficult to show visually, so the publication treats words as another kind of image. “Sweet,” “tart,” “tangy,” “mild,” and “aromatic” are compact descriptors, but they direct expectation. A reader cannot taste the printed apple, yet the vocabulary creates a sensory map around it.

The New York Apple Association frequently places sweetness and tartness together rather than treating them as opposites that cannot coexist. Braeburn is sweet and tangy. Empire is sweet-tart. Honeycrisp is honey sweet with a touch of tart. Granny Smith is described through a balance of tart and sweet. SweeTango is crunchy and sweet with citrus, honey, and spice notes. These combinations suggest that variety is not best understood through one scale alone. A single apple can carry several descriptors at once.

Penn State’s table reinforces the same point in a more systematic format. Some varieties receive marks in both the sweet and tart columns. Ginger Gold, Jonagold, and Braeburn are examples. A category such as “sweet” therefore does not erase acidity, firmness, or juiciness. The table makes the overlap visible.

For the publication, this layered vocabulary is more useful than assigning each apple one personality. A variety can be sweet and tart, firm and juicy, crisp and aromatic. The words can be arranged around images as a set of coordinates rather than a slogan. In that sense, taste becomes a form of classification similar to shape: not perfect, but useful for comparison.

Cut

From Whole to Section

The act of cutting appears in more than one of the five sources, but it serves different purposes. In Michele Warmund’s “Apple Fruit Shapes,” a mature apple is cut longitudinally in half through the core so that its shape can be categorized. In Penn State Extension’s “Let’s Preserve: Apples,” cutting belongs to preparation: apples are washed, peeled, cored, and sliced before freezing or canning. In both cases, the whole fruit becomes easier to examine or use after it is divided.

A longitudinal cut passes through the core and turns the apple into a section. Warmund’s description is brief, but the operation is visually important. The outside silhouette is no longer the only information available. The cut creates a central plane through which the relation between the two sides of the fruit can be seen.

For this publication, the longitudinal section becomes a transition between the earlier study of shape and the later study of preparation. The whole apple presents contour; the cut apple presents contour and interior at the same time. The skin becomes an edge around the section rather than the only visible surface.

The purpose is not to invent a new classification beyond the source. Instead, the publication uses the cut to make the source’s method visible. A form that was previously read from the outside is opened so that its internal organization can participate in comparison.

Peel, Core, Slice

Penn State’s preservation guide gives a more procedural sequence. For several preparations, apples are washed, peeled, cored, and sliced. For freezing sliced apples, the guide instructs the reader to wash, peel, and core the fruit, then divide medium apples into twelfths and large apples into sixteenths. For canned sliced apples, it specifies wedges about one-half inch thick.

These instructions introduce scale and repetition. One apple becomes many pieces, and the pieces are made similar enough to be handled consistently. The whole fruit is no longer the final unit. The slice becomes the working unit.

That change is useful for the book’s visual structure. A whole apple occupies one compact area. A series of wedges or slices can be repeated, aligned, separated, or compared. Cutting therefore changes both the material object and the possibilities for organizing it on the page.

Marks

Traces of Handling

A mark on an apple can be small, but in postharvest guidance it matters because damage changes how the fruit should be handled. Penn State Extension’s article on fruit rots warns that bruised or wounded fruit are susceptible to blue mold and gray mold. The article advises careful handling during harvest and transfer from bag to bin in order to avoid bruising and wounding. Penn State’s “Let’s Preserve: Apples” gives a similar practical instruction at the household scale: heavily bruised apples or apples showing signs of decay should be discarded before preservation. These two sources turn a bruise from a purely visual detail into evidence that the condition of the fruit has changed.

A bruise is different from a clean cut. The outer contour of the apple may remain almost unchanged, while the surface records pressure or impact. In the publication, that makes the bruise useful as a quieter kind of mark. It does not divide the fruit into obvious pieces, but it interrupts the idea of an untouched surface.

The source material does not ask the reader to admire the bruise. It treats bruising as something to prevent. Penn State recommends handling fruit carefully when picking and moving it, precisely because bruised or wounded apples are more vulnerable to postharvest rots. This practical concern gives the visual mark a second meaning: it is not only a change in appearance, but also a sign that the fruit may no longer store in the same way as an undamaged apple.

Instead of many specimens, there is one specimen recorded repeatedly. Each state belongs to the same fruit, yet each image contains information that the previous one did not. The apple becomes a sequence: fresh, exposed, browned, softened, dried.

Time

Storage, Browning, and Change

Time enters the publication most clearly after the apple has been prepared, handled, and stored. The University of Minnesota Extension article on apple storage disorders describes storage as an interaction between the environment, the condition of the apple at harvest, and metabolic changes that continue after harvest.

Penn State’s preservation guide approaches the same subject from a different scale, offering methods for slowing discoloration and maintaining prepared apples during freezing and canning. Together, the two sources show how temperature, humidity, variety, and storage duration affect the apple, which does not remain visually or materially fixed after harvest.

The change is produced after the fruit is opened and its internal tissues are exposed to oxygen. Enzymatic reactions begin to alter the appearance of the flesh. Although the basic structure remains recognizable, the visual character of the section changes continuously. A freshly cut surface looks clean and uniform; later it becomes mottled, darker, and less visually stable.

This transformation also changes the meaning of the image. Earlier in the publication, the apple was compared through proportion, shape, cultivar, and cut. Here, the same object is compared with itself. The difference no longer comes from placing several apples side by side, but from observing one apple at different moments.

Time therefore produces another kind of collection. Instead of many specimens, there is one specimen recorded repeatedly. Each state belongs to the same fruit, yet each image contains information that the previous one did not. The apple becomes a sequence: fresh, exposed, browned, softened, dried.

Color, Browning, and Change

The University of Minnesota article identifies diffuse flesh browning as a storage disorder in which brown discoloration spreads through the flesh. It links the disorder to storage temperatures that are colder than an apple can tolerate and notes that larger fruit may be more likely to develop it. The same article also describes soft scald, in which dark, sunken areas develop on the skin and can extend into the flesh.

Penn State’s preservation guide addresses a different kind of darkening in prepared fruit. When apples are peeled, cored, and sliced, the guide recommends treatments such as ascorbic acid solutions to help prevent discoloration or darkening. The purpose in the guide is practical: preserve the appearance and quality of slices before freezing or canning.

The publication keeps these situations distinct. It does not treat all browning as the same phenomenon. Instead, the repeated appearance of discoloration across the sources becomes a visual theme: color can record that an apple has been cut, stored, or exposed to conditions that affect its quality.
